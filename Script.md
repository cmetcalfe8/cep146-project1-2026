## How it was done (CARTER)


Scientists reconstructed the fruit fly brain by first preserving the brain and cutting it into thousands of extremely thin slices.
Each of these slices were then photographed using a microscope, which created highly detailed images of the brain’s cells and structures.
Next, AI and computer software were used to trace individual neurons across the different images and combine them into a 3D model.
Researchers also mapped the synapses, which are the connections between neurons, and manually checked the AI’s work to correct any mistakes.
AI was especially important in this process because the brain images contained millions of tiny structures.
Tracing all of these by hand would have taken researchers an extremely long time. The software made it much faster to identify neurons and
follow them from one brain slice to the next. Once the AI had completed most of the tracing, scientists reviewed and corrected the results. 
In the end, they created a detailed wiring diagram of the fruit fly brain, known as a connectome.

## History (MAKS AND NIKITA)

Mapping a fruit fly’s brain was like assembling a tiny, complicated puzzle. Researchers had to follow the paths of
individual nerve cells through thousands of images.
In 2018, a team of scientists published a detailed image dataset covering an adult fly’s entire brain. They prepared 
thousands of extremely thin slices, photographed them using electron microscopes, and combined the images into a 
digital three-dimensional model. This allowed researchers to trace cells across different parts of the brain.
However, collecting images was only one stage. Scientists also needed to distinguish neighbouring cells and correctly 
follow their branches. A mistake when connecting fragments could make two separate cells appear to be one.
FlyWire provided a shared online platform where contributors could examine these reconstructions, correct mistakes, 
and see their changes in three dimensions. People in different locations could work on the same project through their 
browsers.
The history of this work shows why scientific software matters: collecting a large dataset is useful, but researchers also
need practical tools to explore, check, and improve it together.
